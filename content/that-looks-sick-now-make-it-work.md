Okay, story time.

From a very early age, probably around 6th or 7th grade, all of a sudden people in my class started drawing tattoo designs in their books and all.

And copying them, I started doing it too.

Somehow, that led me to drawing a lot more things. I actually enjoyed drawing.

It was almost like I would just zone in and keep drawing without really thinking about anything else.

Fast forward to my first year of college.

I discovered Figma, and after seeing people design things in Figma, it felt almost like drawing, just digitally.

Then I started looking at Dribbble, Pinterest, and many other platforms where people were making these super neat mockups.

I used to look at them and think:

> "Man, these designs are so sick. Why don't people actually make these websites? Why don't people use these designs?"

I mean, look at them.

Everything looked so clean.

I'll drop a few images of designs I made as well.

I designed all of these with my own fingers.

Please respect the artist and say nothing.

## Then I Started Building

Fast forward again.

I learned Figma, then started learning web development.

I started making portfolio websites, random projects, and countless other things that I designed but never actually developed.

But after working on real products, I think I finally understand why a lot of those beautiful mockups don't work in most cases.

A design for a fixed screen can look amazing.

But then try making that same thing work on every screen size.

Now you have to deal with:

* Bad network conditions
* Different devices
* Weird user behavior
* Edge cases
* Performance

And don't even get me started on performance.

## When the Design Meets Reality

You might have planned this really cool animation for desktop, but then some user opens your website on their phone.

Suddenly, half of those animations are gone.

That perfectly spaced layout?

Gone.

That beautiful font size?

Maybe way too small on a phone.

That spacing that looked perfect on your 1440px monitor?

Now looks completely different on a mobile screen.

And that's before we even talk about what happens when the user does something you never expected.

That's where the difference between **designing something** and **building something** becomes very obvious.

## Function Over Aesthetics

It's not like people working in these giant companies don't understand design or can't make beautiful things.

They absolutely can.

But in a lot of cases, they choose functionality over design.

If making the font a little simpler and bigger and killing that perfectly equal spacing makes the product easier to use, they'll happily do it.

Because they want the thing to work.

And honestly, I understand that much more now.

For landing pages, things can be different.

You have much more room to experiment and make things visually interesting.

But for a web application, you start seeing a lot more simple designs.

And I think that's intentional.

## It's Not About What I Like

For example, I personally like dark gray.

Something around `gray-700`.

But we had someone complain that they couldn't properly see the gray, so I changed it to solid white.

Do I like it?

No.

It's way too bright for my personal taste.

But the product isn't being made for my personal taste.

We're making it for the user.

And that's the part I think I understand much better now.

## Sometimes "Old" Is Actually Good

I've seen some websites that straight up look like they're from the early 2000s.

At first, you're probably wondering:

> "Why does this look so old?"

It's not necessarily because the people building it can't make a fancy layout.

They probably can.

But maybe they understand their users.

Maybe the person using that software is some old corporate guy who is already tired of learning new things.

They don't want fancy animations.

They don't want some complicated interface.

They just want to get one simple task done.

So the company keeps the product simple.

Maybe they make small adjustments over the years, but they don't completely redesign everything just because a newer design trend came out.

And honestly, I completely agree with that.

## I Still Love Beautiful Designs

I still love beautiful designs.

I still save them.

I still look at them and think:

> "Damn, that's cool."

I just don't look at them the same way anymore.

Because now, somewhere in the back of my head, there's another voice going:

> "Cool. But what happens when someone opens this on an ancient laptop with bad internet and somehow manages to click everything at once?"

I guess that's what building real products does to you.
